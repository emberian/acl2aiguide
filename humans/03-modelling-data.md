# 3. Modelling data

This chapter is about the shape of the data an ACL2 model computes on, and
about the two kinds of efficiency ACL2 lets you buy without leaving the
logic: guard-verified raw-Lisp execution, and destructive update behind a
single-threaded discipline. It covers records, guards, `mbe` and the loop
twin, `defexec`, fixed-width arithmetic, concrete single-threaded objects,
local and nested stobjs, abstract stobjs, attachable stobjs, and
memoization. Chapter 4 builds systems out of these pieces.

Every section opens with what goes wrong and how it looks in the prover's
output, then the mechanism, then the shape to copy. Each shape is a book
under `examples/` that certified on the laptop ACL2 (8.7 on SBCL 2.6.8)
with `examples/certify.sh` before it was cited.

How to read the citations. `[:doc X]` is the ACL2 8.7 manual topic X.
`[books: P]` is a community book under `~/tools/acl2-fn/acl2-8.7/books`.
`[fn: P:L @3bbfc4dca]` is a file of the fn repository at `origin/dev`

## 3.1 Records

### What goes wrong

A record in ACL2 is, underneath, a cons structure. The prover sees through
any function you do not hide, so the first failure mode is that a goal
stated in accessor vocabulary stops being in accessor vocabulary. A rule
about `(art-id x)` cannot fire on a goal where `art-id` has already opened
to `(car (cdr x))`. The same failure appears with generated functions you
did not write: a `defstobj` accessor is an enabled definition over `nth`,
so a lemma stated about `(regsi k mach)` never fires, because the goal
already shows `(nth k (car mach))`
[example: examples/stobjs/array-stobj.lisp]. The checkpoint that tells you
this is a goal full of `car`, `cdr` and `nth` where you expected your own
function names.

The second failure mode is the opposite one: you close the record, and
facts that type reasoning used to supply for free disappear. fn measured it
directly: while `fn-article-msgid` opened to `(car x)`, type reasoning gave
`(consp a)` from `(stringp (fn-article-msgid a))`; withdrawing the
definitions withdrew that fact, and an includer that projected a field out
of a found record failed for want of `(consp a)`
[fn: docs/proof-style.md:151-160 @3bbfc4dca].

Third, a constructor and a shape predicate can disagree, and nothing
complains until you state the lemma that ties them. In the example below
the constructor was first written with tag `:msg` and the shape predicate
with tag `:art`; the shape lemma `art-shapep-of-make-art` then failed with
the goal reduced to `NIL` ("A goal of NIL, Goal', has been generated!")
[measured: `(ld "examples/modelling/opaque-record.lisp")` before the tag
was corrected]. The record lemmas are teeth for the record itself.

Fourth, names. ACL2 has a large built-in vocabulary. The first draft of
that example named its recognizer `msgp` and was refused: "The name MSGP is
in use as a function ... MSGP has already been defined as a system name"
[measured: same]. A `defstobj` field named `pc` is refused the same way,
because `pc` is a macro ("The name PC is in use as a macro")
[measured: `(ld "examples/stobjs/array-stobj.lisp")` before the field was
renamed]; `defstobj`'s `:renaming` exists for exactly this [:doc defstobj].

Fifth, an injectivity theorem for a constructor, left as a global rewrite
rule, fires on every equality between two constructor calls in every book
that includes yours. fn generates injectivity as `:rule-classes nil`, "a
name for one includer's `:use` rather than a rewrite rule that fires on
every constructor equality" [fn: docs/proof-style.md:137-140 @3bbfc4dca].

### The three ways to write a record

**Raw lists under an opaque discipline.** A shape predicate, a constructor,
total accessors, one lemma per field, and the definitions disabled under a
name. This is fn's choice, now generated by its `fn-defrecord` macro
[fn: docs/proof-style.md:30-50 @3bbfc4dca]. What `fn-defrecord` generates,
in fn's own words: "The accessors are total (`:guard t`) with an `mbe`
whose `:logic` is the raw selector and whose `:exec` is the total `fn-ag-`
helper. Immediately after the constructor come the shape lemma and one
accessor-of-constructor lemma per field; then the `:definition` runes of
the shape, the accessors and the constructor are withdrawn under the name
`<record>-internals`. Nothing below that point opens a record: rules are
stated in accessor vocabulary and goals stay in it"
[fn: docs/proof-style.md:81-88 @3bbfc4dca].

Three details of that discipline are each there for a measured reason.

- Withdraw only the `:definition` rune. "The constructor's
  type-prescription (it is a cons) and every executable counterpart stay, so
  `(null (fn-make-pending ...))` still decides and ground evaluation still
  works" [fn: docs/proof-style.md:116-121 @3bbfc4dca].
- Export back, as `:forward-chaining` rules only, the three shape facts
  opacity took away: the shape implies `consp` and `true-listp`; each
  non-nil accessor value implies `consp` of the record; the recognizer
  implies the shape [fn: docs/proof-style.md:158-178 @3bbfc4dca]. "Losing
  one of these three by hand is how the tree bought four rule fans in one
  cycle, which is the whole argument for generating them"
  [fn: docs/proof-style.md:180-182 @3bbfc4dca].
- Write the recognizer over the shape predicate and the accessors, never
  over `car` and `len`: "a `len` conjunct is a `len`-backchaining
  invitation every time the recognizer opens"
  [fn: docs/proof-style.md:133-135 @3bbfc4dca].

Here is the whole discipline, written by hand for a three-field record
[example: examples/modelling/opaque-record.lisp]:

```lisp
(defun ag-car (x) (declare (xargs :guard t)) (if (consp x) (car x) nil))
(defun ag-cdr (x) (declare (xargs :guard t)) (if (consp x) (cdr x) nil))

(defun art-shapep (x)
  (declare (xargs :guard t))
  (and (true-listp x) (equal (len x) 4) (equal (car x) :art)))

(defun art-id (x)
  (declare (xargs :guard t :verify-guards nil))
  (mbe :logic (car (cdr x)) :exec (ag-car (ag-cdr x))))
(verify-guards art-id)
; ... art-body, art-groups the same way ...

(defun make-art (id body groups)
  (declare (xargs :guard t))
  (list :art id body groups))

(defthm art-shapep-of-make-art
  (art-shapep (make-art id body groups)))
(defthm art-id-of-make-art
  (equal (art-id (make-art id body groups)) id))
; ... one lemma per field ...

(defthm art-shapep-forward-shape
  (implies (art-shapep x) (and (consp x) (true-listp x)))
  :rule-classes :forward-chaining)

(defthm art-accessors-forward-consp
  (and (implies (art-id x) (consp x))
       (implies (art-body x) (consp x))
       (implies (art-groups x) (consp x)))
  :rule-classes ((:forward-chaining :corollary (implies (art-id x) (consp x))
                                    :trigger-terms ((art-id x)))
                 ...))

(defthm make-art-injective
  (implies (equal (make-art id1 body1 groups1) (make-art id2 body2 groups2))
           (and (equal id1 id2) (equal body1 body2) (equal groups1 groups2)))
  :rule-classes nil)

(deftheory art-internals
  '((:d art-shapep) (:d art-id) (:d art-body) (:d art-groups) (:d make-art)))
(in-theory (disable art-internals))
```

The recognizer is then written in accessor vocabulary, a transition is
proved without opening the record, and the one form that must open it
names the internals theory in its own hint:

```lisp
(defun artp (x)
  (declare (xargs :guard t))
  (and (art-shapep x)
       (stringp (art-id x))
       (stringp (art-body x))
       (string-listp* (art-groups x))
       (consp (art-groups x))))

(defthm artp-of-art-crosspost
  (implies (and (stringp g) (artp m))
           (artp (art-crosspost g m))))

(defthm art-shapep-is-len-4
  (implies (art-shapep x) (equal (len x) 4))
  :hints (("Goal" :in-theory (enable art-internals)))
  :rule-classes nil)
```

The record lemmas print `Non-rec` warnings when they are admitted ("A
:REWRITE rule generated from ART-SHAPEP-OF-MAKE-ART will be triggered only
by terms containing the function symbols ART-SHAPEP and MAKE-ART, which
have non-recursive definitions") [measured: `(ld
"examples/modelling/opaque-record.lisp")`]. The warning is correct and
expected: the definitions are disabled a few forms later, and from then on
the rules are the only way the prover knows anything about the record.

**`std::defaggregate`.** "Defaggregate introduces a recognizer,
constructor, and accessors for a new record-like structure. It is similar
to `struct` in C or `defstruct` in Lisp" [books: std/util/defaggregate.lisp:47-55].
It writes the same lemmas a hand discipline would, with three differences
worth knowing before choosing it. Its layout is a tagged alist, not a
positional list: the constructor builds `(:POST (ID . id) (BODY . body)
(GROUPS . groups))` and each accessor is an `ASSOC`; a host that reads
records positionally does not read this layout
[example: examples/modelling/defaggregate-define.lisp]. It disables the
recognizer, the constructor and the accessors itself (`(IN-THEORY (DISABLE
POST-P))` and so on in its expansion) and its field-type rules are
`FORCE`d: `(IMPLIES (FORCE (POST-P X)) (STRINGP (POST->ID X)))`
[measured: `:pcb! :x` after the `defaggregate` in that book]. And it
depends on `std/util`, which is the include-cost question below.

**`std::define` with `:returns`.** `define` is "a very fine alternative to
`defun`" with "a concise syntax for naming return values, documenting them,
and proving basic theorems (e.g., type-like theorems) about them"
[books: std/util/define.lisp:71-92]; `:returns` "provides a concise way to
name, document, and prove basic type-like theorems about the return values
of your functions" [books: std/util/returnspecs.lisp:36-46]. By default
"the function will be disabled after the `other-events` are processed"
[books: std/util/define.lisp:216-220], which is the opaque discipline
applied to functions:

```lisp
(define crosspost ((g stringp) (p post-p))
  :returns (new post-p :hyp :guard :name post-p-of-crosspost)
  (change-post p :groups (cons g (post->groups p))))
```

[example: examples/modelling/defaggregate-define.lisp]

**`fty::defprod`.** Not demonstrated here: of `centaur/fty`'s books only
four are certified on this laptop (`basetypes`, `fixequiv`, `fixtype`,
`portcullis`; `ls ~/tools/acl2-fn/acl2-8.7/books/centaur/fty/*.cert`), so
an example would not meet the guide's certification rule. The idea behind
it is Swords and Davis's: "many theorems require type hypotheses. These
hypotheses slow down the theorem prover, are tedious to write, and are easy
to forget. We describe a principled approach to types that provides strong
type safety and execution efficiency while avoiding type hypotheses"
[paper: swords2015-acl2-fix-your-types.txt, abstract].

### What fn chose and why

fn kept raw lists under the opaque discipline and wrote the reasoning down
as "the FTY question" [fn: docs/proof-style.md:709-745 @3bbfc4dca]. What
`fty` would buy: "fixing functions and `-equiv` congruences for free, a
generated `-p` recognizer with the shape baked in, `b*` binders, and the
accessor-of-constructor and constructor-of-accessors lemmas generated
rather than written". What it costs: "every field type needs a fixer and
every theorem inherits `-fix` normal forms; `include-book
"centaur/fty/top"` pulls `std/lists`, `std/util` and their enabled rules
into every book above, which is exactly the enabled-rule pollution this
document exists to stop; the host encodes records as positional lists
today ... and `defprod` layouts are `:layout :list`-compatible only when
asked" [fn: docs/proof-style.md:719-730 @3bbfc4dca]. Once `fn-defrecord`
existed, "the 'generated rather than written' half of what `fty` would buy
is bought by `fn-defrecord`, at no include cost and with no fixing
functions in keystone statements. What remains genuinely `fty`-only is
congruence reasoning over record equivalences"
[fn: docs/proof-style.md:732-735 @3bbfc4dca].

The problem the discipline solved was measured before it was adopted: "no
typed structures (806 raw-list accessors), 642 `-is-`/`-of-` equalities of
which 68 were `:rule-classes nil` and the rest were global rewrite rules on
include, whole-state recognizers recomputed on every served operation ...
Each of those is a symptom of the same thing: books exported their
internals" [fn: docs/proof-style.md:21-28 @3bbfc4dca].

fn's wound here is not the discipline but its reach. The records stayed
cons tuples even where a concrete representation was the point: in the
2026-10-28 representation history the owner, view and store node are "15-,
14- and 9-element cons tuples, lists of connections and feeds, car/cdr
chains", read by chains such as `fn-sf-records-field` whose `:exec` is
`fn-ag-car`/`fn-ag-cdr`
[fn: build/coordinator/REPRESENTATION-HISTORY-20261028.md:68,178 @worktree 2026-10-28].
An opaque raw-list record is a good logical interface. It is not a
runtime representation, and the two decisions need to be made separately.

### The export theory

The record's lemmas stay enabled on include; its recognizer, its
transitions and its proof vocabulary do not. fn's rule: "Every book ends
with an explicit theory event. It withdraws the recognizers of records and
states, the initial state, the transitions and every lemma that is proof
vocabulary rather than a keystone"
[fn: docs/proof-style.md:184-190 @3bbfc4dca]; and the list of what may and
may not leave a book enabled is at [fn: docs/proof-style.md:517-534 @3bbfc4dca].
The example book ends `(in-theory (disable artp art-crosspost))`
[example: examples/modelling/opaque-record.lisp].

## 3.2 Guards

### What goes wrong

**A guard is not a hypothesis.** "A definition's guard has no effect on the
logical axiom added when that definition is accepted by ACL2. Moreover, the
guard has NO EFFECT on the proof that a recursive definition is admissible"
[:doc guard]. So every theorem about a guarded function must carry, as
hypotheses, whatever part of the guard it depends on. The 32-bit rotate in
`examples/modelling/fixed-width.lisp` is guarded by `(type (integer 0 31)
n)`, but logically it is defined for every `n`, and for `n = 40` its second
shift is a left shift: the type theorem without `(natp n) (< n 32)` is
false, and the book carries an `assert-event` that evaluates the
counterexample under `(with-guard-checking :none ...)`
[example: examples/modelling/fixed-width.lisp].

**Guard verification fails the way any proof fails**, with a checkpoint,
and the checkpoint is usually a fact about a recursive function that the
prover could not invent. Defining

```lisp
(defun checksum32-nohint (xs)
  (declare (xargs :guard (and (byte-listp* xs) (< (len xs) 1000000))))
  (the (unsigned-byte 32) (sum-bytes xs)))
```

prints the guard conjecture

```
(IMPLIES (AND (< (LEN XS) 1000000)
              (BYTE-LISTP* XS))
         (LET ((VAR (SUM-BYTES XS)))
           (UNSIGNED-BYTE-P 32 VAR)))
```

and fails with the key checkpoint `(< (SUM-BYTES XS) 4294967296)` and, under
the induction it tried, a goal reduced to `(IMPLIES (< (+ 1 (LEN XS2))
1000000) (NOT (BYTE-LISTP* XS2)))`, which is false
[measured: the definition above after `(ld "examples/modelling/guards.lisp")`].
The induction cannot work: the bound `(<= (sum-bytes xs) (* 255 (len xs)))`
is the fact needed, and it is not in the goal.

**A guard-verified function may not call one whose guards are not
verified.** "The body for REVERSE-AGREES-DIRECT calls the function
SPEC-REVERSE, the guards of which have not yet been verified. See :DOC
verify-guards" [measured: a direct call of `spec-reverse` from a `:guard t`
function, after loading `examples/modelling/guards.lisp`]. The way out,
when you mean it, is `ec-call` (below).

**A guarded theorem cannot be stated with `implies`.** The manual's own
example: "`implies` is defined as a function in ACL2. When it is called,
both arguments are evaluated ... Hence, when we write the guarded theorem
in the `implies` form we have to prove the guards on the conclusion without
knowing that the hypothesis is true" [:doc verify-guards]. Guard-verifying
a theorem, which proves that it evaluates to true in every Common Lisp
without relying on ACL2's completion of partial functions, needs the `if`
form (or `defthmg`) [:doc verify-guards].

### What a guard is

"Guards can be seen as having either of two roles: (a) they are a
specification device allowing you to characterize the kinds of inputs a
function 'should' have, or (b) they are an efficiency device allowing
logically defined functions to be executed directly in Common Lisp. ... If
the guards of a function definition are 'verified' ..., then the
evaluation of a call of that function on arguments satisfying its guard
will have the following property: All subsequent function calls during
that evaluation will be on arguments satisfying the guard of the called
function" [:doc guard-introduction].

Every ACL2 function has two Common Lisp definitions: "the
'executable-counterpart' definition, sometimes called the '*1*' function
...; and the 'submitted' definition (sometimes called the 'raw Lisp'
function)" [:doc evaluation]. The executable counterpart checks guards and
calls other executable counterparts; once a function's guards are verified,
its executable counterpart calls the submitted definition when the guard
holds. The manual's traces show both cases. With `f` and `g` unverified,
`(g '(4 5 6))` runs `ACL2_*1*_ACL2::G` then `ACL2_*1*_ACL2::F`; with both
verified, it runs `ACL2_*1*_ACL2::G`, then `G`, then `F` -- raw Lisp all the
way down [:doc evaluation].

What runs where depends also on the guard-checking setting, in a table of
three rows and four columns [:doc guard-evaluation-table]. The rows that
matter in practice:

- a `:logic` function whose guards are not verified, at the default
  setting `t`: "Guards are checked at the top-level, though not at
  self-recursive calls. We never run the raw Lisp code in this case; guards
  would need to be verified first" (row b1);
- a guard-verified `:logic` function at `t` or `:all`: "Guards are checked.
  If the checks pass, evaluation takes place using the raw Lisp code"
  (rows c1, c2);
- at `:none`, for user-defined guard-verified functions "no guard checking
  is done, and the only time the raw Lisp code will be executed is when the
  guard is `t` and guards are verified at the time the
  executable-counterpart of the function is defined" (row c3); "If you
  want the speed of executing raw Lisp code and you have non-trivial guards
  on functions that you want to call at the top-level, use `nil` rather
  than `:none`"
  [:doc guard-evaluation-table].

Note also that "`'guard-checking-on` is set to `nil` during proofs but is
set to `t` during `certify-book`, and `include-book`, regardless of how
this variable has been set in the top-level loop"
[:doc guard-evaluation-table].

fn states the consequence for a served system in one line: "The executed
path is what counts" [fn: docs/proof-style.md:869 @3bbfc4dca]. "A
guard-verified body and anything below a `:program` host wrapper run raw
Lisp. There `mbe` runs `:exec` and no callee guard is evaluated. A native
dispatch `(fnn-core 'f ...)` calls f's executable counterpart, so **f's
guard is evaluated on every call**. A whole-state recognizer in the guard
of a function the host calls is therefore a walk per request. ... A
function whose guards are not verified, entered from a counterpart, runs
its `*1*` body. There `mbe` runs `:logic` and every callee's guard is
evaluated" [fn: docs/proof-style.md:871-879 @3bbfc4dca]. The guard is thus
both a proof obligation and, at the entry point, a run-time cost.

### When guard verification is attempted

"Typically, guard verification takes place at definition time if a guard
(or type, or stobjs) has been supplied explicitly unless `:verify-guards
nil` has been specified" [:doc verify-guards]. The default is controlled by
`set-verify-guards-eagerness`: 0 never tries unless `:verify-guards t`, 1
tries "if `:guard`, type `:stobjs`, or `:df` present", 2 tries unless
`:verify-guards nil`, 3 always; "The default behavior of the system is as
though the `:verify-guards-eagerness` is `1`", and the setting is local to
books and encapsulates because it is a table event [:doc set-verify-guards-eagerness].

This matters for anything that reads guard status from source. fn's ledger
distinguishes `verified`, `declared-off`, `default-guarded` ("an explicit
`:guard` or `type` declaration, which under the default
`set-verify-guards-eagerness` of 1 means ACL2 verified guards at definition
time") and `default-unguarded`, and "Only `verified` is an observation;
`default-guarded` is an inference about what ACL2 did"
[fn: docs/proofs.md:186-197 @3bbfc4dca].

### The obligations

"Each function call is considered in the body or guard of the function, and
it is required that the guard is met for that call, under certain
'contextual assumptions' ... In the case of the body of the named function,
it is assumed that the guard holds for that function on its formal
parameters. And in both cases -- the body of the named function and also
its guard -- the governing tests from superior calls of IF are also
assumed" [:doc verify-guards]. The guard itself must be guard-safe: "guard
verification fails in the following example, even though there are no
proof obligations arising from the body, because the guard itself can cause
a guard violation when evaluated for an arbitrary value of `x`:
`(defun foo (x) (declare (xargs :guard (car x))) x)`" [:doc verify-guards].

A `THE` form contributes its type as an obligation: "A call of `(the TYPE
EXPR)` in the body of a function definition generates a guard proof
obligation that the type, `TYPE`, holds for the value of the expression,
`EXPR`" [:doc the]. A type declaration on a formal becomes a guard conjunct:
"a type-spec of `(unsigned-byte i)` for a variable `x` in a function's
`declare` form translates to a guard condition of `(unsigned-byte-p i x)`"
[:doc unsigned-byte-p].

### The shapes that close them

**Prove the lemma first, then verify.** The manual's `rev` example: define
with `:verify-guards nil`, prove `(implies (true-listp x2) (true-listp (rev
x2)))`, then `(verify-guards rev)`; "in many more complicated functions it
is necessary for the user to formulate the inductively provable properties
before guard verification is attempted" [:doc verify-guards].

**A guard fact is a `:rule-classes nil` lemma named in `:guard-hints`.**
fn's convention: "An equality that exists to discharge a guard or an `mbe`
obligation is not a rewrite rule. It is stated once, `:rule-classes nil`,
and used by `:use`" [fn: docs/proof-style.md:242-260 @3bbfc4dca]. The
checksum above, done that way [example: examples/modelling/guards.lisp]:

```lisp
(defthm sum-bytes-bound
  (implies (byte-listp* xs)
           (and (natp (sum-bytes xs))
                (<= (sum-bytes xs) (* 255 (len xs)))))
  :rule-classes nil)

(defun checksum32 (xs)
  (declare (xargs :guard (and (byte-listp* xs) (< (len xs) 1000000))
                  :guard-hints (("Goal" :use ((:instance sum-bytes-bound))))))
  (the (unsigned-byte 32) (sum-bytes xs)))
```

**`:guard-debug` names the call each obligation came from.** "`(Extra-info
x y)` always returns `t` by definition. However, if guard verification
takes place with a non-`nil` setting of `guard-debug` ..., then the goals
generated for guard verification can include hypotheses that are calls of
`extra-info`" of the form `(extra-info '(:guard (:body F)) '(G ARG1 ...
ARGk))`, and "These hypotheses are intended to ride along for free"
[:doc guard-debug]. For the checksum, `(verify-guards checksum32-dbg
:guard-debug t)` prints

```
(IMPLIES (AND (< (LEN XS) 1000000)
              (BYTE-LISTP* XS))
         (LET ((VAR (SUM-BYTES XS)))
           (OR (NOT (EXTRA-INFO '(:GUARD (:BODY CHECKSUM32-DBG))
                                '(THE-CHECK (UNSIGNED-BYTE-P 32 VAR)
                                            '(UNSIGNED-BYTE 32)
                                            VAR)))
               (UNSIGNED-BYTE-P 32 VAR))))
```

and the failed subgoals carry the same `EXTRA-INFO` hypothesis, so a
checkpoint in a large body points at its `THE`
[measured: `checksum32-dbg`, as in the book but without the hint].

**A `:use`d fact can be erased by its own rewrite rule.** In
`examples/stobjs/nested-stobj.lisp` a fact `(implies (acctp a) (and
(integerp (bal a)) (<= 0 (bal a))))` was first stored as a rewrite rule and
also given in `:guard-hints :use`. The guard proof failed with the checkpoint
still asking for `(INTEGERP (+ AMT (BAL (ACCTSI I BANK))))`, and the
`:use`d hypothesis visible in it had lost its `INTEGERP` conjunct: the
rewrite rule had simplified the hypothesis to `T` before type reasoning saw
it [measured: the book with the lemma's rule classes `((:rewrite ...)
(:linear ...))`]. The book's comment records the fix: the fact is
`:rule-classes nil` and only `:use`d. And a rewrite rule concluding
`(INTEGERP (BAL A))` would not have helped anyway, because a goal
`(INTEGERP (+ AMT (BAL ...)))` is settled by type reasoning, which does not
call the rewriter [example: examples/stobjs/nested-stobj.lisp].

### `ec-call`, `defun-nx`, `mbt`

`ec-call` executes one call in the logic: "Semantically, `(ec-call (fn
term1 ... termk))` equals `(fn term1 ... termk)`. However, this use of
`ec-call` has two effects. (1) Guard verification generates no proof
obligations from the guard of `fn` for this call. Indeed, guards need not
have been verified for `fn`. (2) During evaluation, after the arguments of
`fn` are evaluated as usual, the executable-counterpart of `fn` is called,
rather than its submitted version" [:doc ec-call]. "The use of `ec-call`
does not turn off guard-checking" [:doc ec-call]. The example compares a
fast accumulator reverse against an unverified specification inside a
guard-verified checker:

```lisp
(defun reverse-agrees-p (x)
  (declare (xargs :guard t))
  (equal (fast-reverse x nil) (ec-call (spec-reverse x))))
```

[example: examples/modelling/guards.lisp]

`defun-nx` defines a function that must never run: "calls of the
resulting function cannot be evaluated. Such a definition is admitted
without enforcing syntactic restrictions for executability, in particular
for single-threadedness ... and multiple-values passing ... After executing
the `defun`, the executable-counterpart rune for `name` is disabled"
[:doc defun-nx]. Evaluating one at the top level says so: "ACL2 cannot ev
the call of non-executable function SOME-PERMUTATION-P on argument list:
((1 2) (2 1))" [measured: after `(ld "examples/modelling/guards.lisp")`].
It is the right home for a specification and for an abstract stobj's
correspondence relation (section 3.9). "If you use guards ... please be
aware that even though syntactic restrictions are relaxed for `defun-nx`,
guard verification proceeds exactly as for `defun`" [:doc defun-nx].

`mbt` puts a guard fact into the logic for the termination proof without
paying for it at run time: "Semantically, `(mbt x)` equals `x`. However, in
raw Lisp `(mbt x)` ignores `x` entirely, and macroexpands to `t`. ACL2's
guard verification mechanism ensures that the raw Lisp code is only
evaluated when appropriate, since a guard proof obligation `(equal x t)` is
generated" [:doc mbt]. The example counts up from `i` to `n` with the
measure `(nfix (- n i))` and the test `(mbt (and (natp i) (natp n) (<= i
n)))` [example: examples/modelling/guards.lisp]. fn's recognizers-as-carried-invariants
use the same idea with `mbe`: "`(if (mbe :logic (not (fn-statep s)) :exec
nil) s ...)`" keeps the total logical definition while the executable path
relies on the guard [fn: docs/proof-style.md:269-283 @3bbfc4dca].

## 3.3 `mbe` and the loop twin

### What goes wrong

The `mbe` equality is a guard obligation, so it is proved when guards are
verified, and only then. Define a function with an `mbe`, leave it with
`:verify-guards nil`, and nothing has been claimed about the `:exec` side
at all. When you verify guards, the obligation appears as an `EQUAL` with
the logic body on one side and the exec body on the other:

```
(IMPLIES
 (AND
    (EXTRA-INFO '(:GUARD (:BODY KEEP-EVENS-2))
                '(MBE :LOGIC (AND (CONSP XS)
                                  (IF (EVENP (CAR XS))
                                      (CONS (CAR XS) (KEEP-EVENS-2 (CDR XS)))
                                    (KEEP-EVENS-2 (CDR XS))))
                      :EXEC (KEEP-EVENS-LOOP XS NIL)))
    (INTEGER-LISTP XS))
 (EQUAL (AND (CONSP XS)
             (IF (EVENP (CAR XS))
                 (CONS (CAR XS) (KEEP-EVENS-2 (CDR XS)))
               (KEEP-EVENS-2 (CDR XS))))
        (KEEP-EVENS-LOOP XS NIL)))
```

[measured: `(verify-guards keep-evens-2 :guard-debug t)` for a copy of the
book's `keep-evens`, after loading `examples/modelling/mbe-loop-twin.lisp`]

The goal is about the accumulator at `NIL`, and an induction on it fails:
the recursive call is at a non-`NIL` accumulator. The bridge lemma has to be
stated for every accumulator.

Two further traps. "Termination is not considered for calls of `mbe` under
the top-level call. Moreover, the `:exec` part of an `mbe` call under the
`:logic` part of any superior `mbe` call is completely ignored" [:doc defexec].
And `mbe` can lead to non-terminating computations, which `defexec` exists
to rule out (section 3.4) [:doc mbe].

### The mechanism

"In the ACL2 logic, `(mbe :exec exec-code :logic logic-code)` equals
`logic-code`; the value of `exec-code` is ignored. However, in raw Lisp it
is the other way around: this form macroexpands simply to `exec-code`.
ACL2's guard verification mechanism ensures that the raw Lisp code is only
evaluated when appropriate, since the guard proof obligations generated for
(the macroexpansion of) this call of `mbe` include not only the guard proof
obligations from `exec-code`, but also, under suitable contextual
assumptions, the term `(equal exec-code logic-code)`" [:doc mbe]. Which
side runs: "during evaluation of an `mbe` call, only the `:logic` code is
evaluated unless the call is in the body of a guard-verified function or
under a call of a `:program` mode function; in those cases only the `:exec`
code is evaluated. This implies that equality of `:exec` and `:logic` code
is never checked at runtime" -- with two exceptions, safe mode (both run and
are compared) and guard-checking `:all` or `:none` (only `:logic` runs)
[:doc mbe]. "The `:exec` and the `:logic` code in an `mbe` call must have
the same output signature" [:doc mbe]. In the prover, "the proof obligations
generated for admitting a function treat the above `mbe` term simply as
`logic-code`. Function expansion, `:use` hints, `:definition` rules,
generation of constraints for functional instantiation, and creation of
rules of class `:rewrite` and `:forward-chaining` also treat `mbe` calls as
their `:logic` code" [:doc mbe].

### The loop twin

The shape fn wrote by hand hundreds of times and then generated: "a
structural recursion is the `:logic` body the rewriter sees; an accumulator
loop `NAME-loop` is the `:exec` body that runs in constant stack; a local
bridge `NAME-loop-is-revappend` states that the loop is `revappend` of the
accumulator onto the recursion; and two `verify-guards` close the `mbe`"
[fn: books/def-loop.lisp:5-10 @3bbfc4dca]. The example
[example: examples/modelling/mbe-loop-twin.lisp]:

```lisp
(defun keep-evens-loop (xs acc)
  (declare (xargs :guard (and (integer-listp xs) (true-listp acc))))
  (if (consp xs)
      (keep-evens-loop (cdr xs)
                       (if (evenp (car xs)) (cons (car xs) acc) acc))
    (revappend acc nil)))

(defun keep-evens (xs)
  (declare (xargs :guard (integer-listp xs) :verify-guards nil))
  (mbe :logic (if (consp xs)
                  (if (evenp (car xs))
                      (cons (car xs) (keep-evens (cdr xs)))
                    (keep-evens (cdr xs)))
                nil)
       :exec (keep-evens-loop xs nil)))

(defthm keep-evens-loop-is-revappend
  (equal (keep-evens-loop xs acc)
         (revappend acc (keep-evens xs))))

(verify-guards keep-evens)

(in-theory (disable keep-evens-loop))
```

Every later theorem is about `keep-evens`, the recursion; none mentions the
loop. The book ends by running the guard-verified wrapper on a list of a
million integers, which the loop handles in constant stack.

fn counted the cost of writing this by hand: "twenty-six lines, one
induction under `minimal-theory` and two guard proofs per loop, 850
inductions tree-wide, and every one of them the same induction"
[fn: books/def-loop.lisp:10-13 @3bbfc4dca]. Its `def-loop` proves "each
SHAPE's bridge ... ONCE, over constrained functions that have no axioms",
and "every instance obtains its bridge by `:functional-instance` of that
one theorem", the obligation being "the definitional equation of `NAME` and
of `NAME-loop` under the substitution, which one unfold of each discharges
in `minimal-theory`: no instance induction, no instance hint"
[fn: books/def-loop.lisp:14-20 @3bbfc4dca]. Chapter 4 section 4.6 builds
that generalisation [example: examples/systems/generic-loop.lisp].

The twin is also fn's largest wound in this area, because a twin is two
paths. The strategy review of 2026-10-06 counted "`def-loop` 55 instances,
against 494 hand loop twins and 850 equivalence inductions (~13K lines)"
[fn: build/coordinator/STRATEGY-20261006.md:266 @worktree 2026-10-28],
and the representation history names the same mechanism one level up:
"The design law the waves were written under (D27: 'a correspondence
theorem to the list definition at every boundary the host calls') made
each new buffer path a **twin** of a list path, proved equal to it. The
list path stayed callable, and new host code kept calling it"
[fn: build/coordinator/REPRESENTATION-HISTORY-20261028.md:136 @worktree 2026-10-28].
An `mbe` twin is safe only because nothing can call its `:logic` side at
run time once guards are verified. A twin that is a second callable
function has no such protection.

## 3.4 `defexec`

### What goes wrong

The guard is often exactly what makes a recursion terminate, and "the
definitional principle ... ignores the guard" [:doc defexec]. So the
admissible definition must test the guard itself, and then every recursive
call pays for the test. Putting the test only in the `:logic` branch of an
`mbe` fixes the cost but loses the termination argument for the `:exec`
branch, which `defun` does not check.

### The mechanism

`defexec` "causes a termination proof to be performed, in order to
guarantee that evaluation always theoretically terminates, even when using
the `:exec` form for evaluation" [:doc defexec]. Its expansion is a local
definition of the exec body guarded by the test, plus a local theorem
`(implies guard test)`: "We claim that if the above `local-def` and
`local-thm` are admitted, then all evaluations of calls of `fn` terminate"
[:doc defexec]. The example: the number of base-B digits of N, where
`(floor n b)` decreases only when `b > 1`, which is the guard
[example: examples/modelling/defexec.lisp]:

```lisp
(defexec digit-count (n b)
  (declare (xargs :guard (and (natp n) (integerp b) (< 1 b))
                  :measure (nfix n)))
  (mbe :logic (if (and (natp n) (integerp b) (< 1 b))
                  (if (< n b) 1 (+ 1 (digit-count (floor n b) b)))
                0)
       :exec (if (< n b) 1 (+ 1 (digit-count (floor n b) b)))))
```

Admitting it prints the two local events, `(LOCAL (DEFUN DIGIT-COUNT (N B)
...))` over the exec body and `(LOCAL (DEFTHM DIGIT-COUNT-GUARD-IMPLIES-TEST
...))` [measured: `(ld "examples/modelling/defexec.lisp")`]. Outside the
guard the logic answers its default, and the book checks that `(digit-count
255 1)` is `0` under `with-guard-checking :none`.

The community books carry larger uses under `books/defexec/`
(`dag-unification`, `defpun-exec`, `find-path`, `ordinals`, `other-apps`,
`reflexive`) [books: defexec/]. fn's `def-loop` lists `defexec` among the
shapes it does not generate [fn: books/def-loop.lisp:124-127 @3bbfc4dca].

## 3.5 Fixed-width arithmetic

### What goes wrong

**`unsigned-byte-p` left enabled turns width goals into inequalities the
bit libraries have no rules for.** The rotate below, with
`centaur/bitops/ihsext-basics` included and `unsigned-byte-p` enabled,
fails its guard proof at

```
(IMPLIES (AND (<= N 31) (<= 0 N) (INTEGERP N)
              (INTEGERP X) (<= 0 X) (< X 4294967296))
         (< (LOGIOR (LOGHEAD 32 (ASH X N))
                    (LOGTAIL (+ 32 (- N)) X))
            4294967296))
```

With `unsigned-byte-p` disabled the same proof closes in 580 steps using
`UNSIGNED-BYTE-P-OF-LOGIOR`, `UNSIGNED-BYTE-P-OF-LOGHEAD` and
`UNSIGNED-BYTE-P-OF-LOGTAIL` [measured: the `rotl32` definition of
`examples/modelling/fixed-width.lisp`, with and without
`:guard-hints (("Goal" :in-theory (disable unsigned-byte-p)))`]. The book
disables it locally after the include.

**The guard is not a hypothesis** (section 3.2): `rotl32`'s type theorem
needs the range of `n` among its hypotheses
[example: examples/modelling/fixed-width.lisp].

**A general arithmetic library can be the slow path.** fn's note: "For
base64, arithmetic-5 was about 10 times slower than a few fixed-modulus
lemmas (lane shared-books). Prefer the small lemmas for fixed moduli"
[fn: docs/proof-style.md:912 @3bbfc4dca]. And one rule-class combination
crashed the host: "A `:linear` rule with `:trigger-terms` and a `:rewrite`
class on the same conclusion crashed SBCL with a memory fault (lane
shared-books, 2026-09-28). Split it into a type rule and a plain `:linear`
rule" [fn: docs/proof-style.md:911 @3bbfc4dca].

### The mechanism

Fixed-width values are ordinary integers with a width predicate:
"`(Unsigned-byte-p bits x)` is `T` when `bits` is a positive integer and
`x` is a nonnegative integer that fits into a bit-width of `bits`, i.e.,
`0 <= x < 2^bits`" [:doc unsigned-byte-p]. The operations are
`logand`/`logior`/`ash` (built in) and `loghead`/`logtail` from IHS, whose
definitions are themselves `mbe` twins of the arithmetic meaning and the
machine operation: `loghead` is `(mbe :logic (imod i (expt2 size)) :exec
(the unsigned-byte (logand i (the unsigned-byte (1- (the unsigned-byte (ash
1 size)))))))` and `logtail` is `(mbe :logic (ifloor i (expt2 pos)) :exec
(ash i (- (the unsigned-byte pos))))` [books: ihs/basic-definitions.lisp:394-433].

`THE` is the bridge to the compiler: "It is usually used as a way to boost
performance by telling the Common Lisp compiler that a certain expression
will always produce a result of a certain type"; the manual's example
merges two bytes into a 16-bit word, and "On most Lisp implementations
16-bit numbers are fixnums. The `THE` forms above are promises to the Lisp
compiler that these `ash` and `logior` operations will always produce
16-bit numbers" [:doc the]. Each such promise is a guard obligation, so the
guard proof is where the width is proved. The example book has that merge,
the 32-bit rotate, and a little-endian packer
[example: examples/modelling/fixed-width.lisp]:

```lisp
(local (in-theory (disable unsigned-byte-p)))

(defun rotl32 (x n)
  (declare (type (unsigned-byte 32) x)
           (type (integer 0 31) n))
  (the (unsigned-byte 32)
       (logior (the (unsigned-byte 32) (loghead 32 (ash x n)))
               (the (unsigned-byte 32) (ash x (- n 32))))))

(defthm rotl32-type
  (implies (and (unsigned-byte-p 32 x) (natp n) (< n 32))
           (unsigned-byte-p 32 (rotl32 x n))))
```

The libraries. IHS "is a collection of arithmetic books, mainly geared
toward bit-vector arithmetic" in which "bit-vectors are represented as
ordinary ACL2 integers" and "the library introduces alternate, list-style,
recursive definitions for operations like `logand`" so that one can induct
over bits [books: ihs/ihs-doc-topic.lisp]. Bitops "grew out of an extension
to the venerable ihs library, and is now fairly comprehensive", providing
single-bit operations, reasoning support for `logand`, `ash`, `logbitp` and
the IHS operations, and "Efficient implementations of certain bit-vector
operations ... with lemmas or `mbe` to relate them to the logically nicer
definitions" [books: centaur/bitops/top.lisp:53-80]. On this laptop the IHS
books and six bitops books are certified, including `ihsext-basics`, which
is what the example includes (`ls
~/tools/acl2-fn/acl2-8.7/books/centaur/bitops/*.cert`).

### Bit-blasting: GL and FGL

GL is "a framework for proving finite ACL2 theorems -- those which, at
least in principle, could be established by exhaustive testing -- by
bit-blasting with a Binary Decision Diagram (BDD) package or a SAT solver"
[paper: swords2011-acl2-bit-blasting-gl.txt, section 1]. It pays when the
property is finite and the reason it holds is not interesting: such tools
"Reduce the level of human understanding needed in the initial process of
developing the proof; Provide clear counterexamples, whereas failed ACL2
proofs can often be difficult to debug; and Ease the maintenance of the
proof" [books: centaur/gl/doc.lisp:38-70]. Its main command takes the
hypothesis and conclusion apart and asks for symbolic bindings of the free
variables:

```lisp
(def-gl-thm fast-logcount-32-correct
  :hyp   (unsigned-byte-p 32 x)
  :concl (equal (fast-logcount-32 x)
                (logcount x))
  :g-bindings `((x ,(g-int 0 1 33))))
```

"This separation allows GL to use the hypothesis to limit the scope of the
symbolic execution it will perform" [books: centaur/gl/tutorial.lisp:377-400].

FGL "is the successor to GL. It mainly consists of a clause processor that
calls on a custom rewriter/term interpreter which features support for
efficient representations of Boolean functions", adding AIGNET-based
Boolean functions and incremental SAT during rewriting; "BDD and hons-AIG
modes are not complete. Shape specifiers don't exist yet"
[books: centaur/fgl/doc.lisp:35-72]. Its theorem form needs no bindings:

```lisp
(fgl::def-fgl-thm my-silly-theorem
  :hyp (unsigned-byte-p 3 x)
  :concl (not (logbitp 4 x)))
```

[books: centaur/fgl/doc.lisp:84-91]; `def-fgl-thm` "produces a `defthm`
form containing hints that cause the FGL clause processor to be used"
[books: centaur/fgl/def-fgl-thm.lisp:108-120]. Its test book has the
fixed-width shapes this section is about, for instance `(fgl-thm :hyp (and
(unsigned-byte-p 50 x) (unsigned-byte-p 50 y)) :concl (unsigned-byte-p 50
(logand x y)))` [books: centaur/fgl/tests.lisp:375-378]. FGL needs an
external SAT solver ("the default for FGL is glucose")
[books: centaur/fgl/doc.lisp:72-74]. Neither GL nor FGL is certified on the
laptop, so this guide has no example of its own; the tests cited are the
community books'.

## 3.6 Single-threaded objects

### What goes wrong

The manual's first advice is to be careful: "Novices are advised to avoid
`defstobj`, perhaps instead using community books `std::defaggregate` or
`books/data-structures/structures.lisp`. At the least, consider using
`(set-verify-guards-eagerness 0)` to avoid guard verification" [:doc defstobj].
The failures that follow are the reasons.

- **Name clashes.** Every field gets several generated names. A field
  named `pc` is refused because `pc` is an ACL2 macro; "Use of the
  `:renaming` doublets may be necessary to avoid name clashes between the
  default names and pre-existing function symbols", and "the renaming is
  applied to the default function names, not to the field descriptors", so
  the updater is renamed separately [:doc defstobj]. The example renames
  `(pc get-pc)` and `(update-pc set-pc)` [example: examples/stobjs/array-stobj.lisp].
- **Generated functions are enabled definitions** over `nth` and
  `update-nth`, so lemmas stated about them do not fire (section 3.1).
  The example proves its read-over-write facts while they are open, then
  closes `regsi`, `update-regsi`, `regs-length` and `machp`
  [example: examples/stobjs/array-stobj.lisp].
- **The recognizer of an array field is a recursion over a list.** The
  preservation fact a caller's guard needs -- an in-bounds, in-type write
  keeps the stobj well formed -- failed first with the checkpoint `(REGSP
  (UPDATE-NTH J V (CAR MACH)))`; the list lemma `regsp-of-update-nth` is
  needed before it [example: examples/stobjs/array-stobj.lisp]. The same
  pair of lemmas (a read is in type; an in-type write keeps the type)
  appears in each array-field example in this guide (`array-stobj`,
  `local-stobj`, `nested-stobj`).
- **A type fact stated as a rewrite rule does not reach a type goal.**
  `(implies (binsp l) (unsigned-byte-p 60 (nth i l)))` as a rewrite rule
  never fires, because `unsigned-byte-p` is enabled and the goal shows `(<
  (NTH I L) 1152921504606846976)`; it works as a `:type-prescription` plus
  a `:linear` rule [example: examples/stobjs/local-stobj.lisp].
- **The stobj argument must be the stobj variable.** "If a function uses
  the formal parameter `OBJ` that is declared as a stobj, the only 'actual
  expression' that can be passed into that slot is the variable `OBJ`, not
  merely a term that 'evaluates to an obj' ... instead of `(FOO
  (UPDATE-FIELD1 3 ST))` write `(LET ((ST (UPDATE-FIELD1 3 ST))) (FOO ST))`"
  [:doc stobj]. The error, when you forget: "The form (COUNT-BYTES XS HIST)
  is being used, as an argument to a call of MOST-COMMON-BYTE-LOOP, where
  the single-threaded object HIST is required. Note that the variable HIST
  is required, not merely a term that returns such a single-threaded
  object, so you may need to bind HIST with LET"
  [measured: the second `must-fail` form of `examples/stobjs/local-stobj.lisp`].
- **A redefinition does not reset.** "A `defstobj` is considered redundant
  only if it is syntactically identical to a previously executed
  `defstobj`. Note that a redundant `defstobj` does not reset the stobj
  fields to their initial values" [:doc defstobj].

### What a stobj is

"In ACL2, a 'single-threaded object' is a data structure whose use is so
syntactically restricted that only one instance of the object need ever
exist and its fields can be updated by destructive assignments" [:doc stobj].
"From the logical perspective, a single-threaded object is an ordinary ACL2
object, e.g., composed of integers and conses. Logically speaking,
ordinary ACL2 functions are defined to allow the user to 'access' and
'update' its fields" [:doc stobj]. The syntactic rules, from the same topic:
the stobj is passed only as its own variable; accessors and updaters take
it as a formal; "The ACL2 primitives, such as `CONS`, `CAR` and `CDR`, may
not be applied to the variable `OBJ`"; "If a function calls an `OBJ`
updater, it must return an `OBJ` object"; and the restrictions "are
enforced only when single-threaded objects are encountered directly in the
top-level loop or are used in function definitions; the accessor and update
functions for single-threaded objects may be used without restriction in
formulas to be proved" [:doc stobj].

### `defstobj`

The general form [:doc defstobj]:

```lisp
(defstobj name
          (field1 :type type1
                  :element-type etype1
                  :initially val1
                  :resizable b1)
          ...
          :renaming doublets
          :inline flg
          :congruent-to old-stobj-name
          :non-memoizable nm-flg
          :non-executable ne-flg)
```

The field types and what they generate:

- A scalar type-spec; `:initially` gives the value, which "is not a form to
  be evaluated but an object": `(days-off :initially (saturday sunday))` is
  the list of two symbols, and quoting it would give a list whose first
  element is `quote` [:doc defstobj].
- `(ARRAY type-spec (max))`: "the field is supposed to be a list of items,
  initially of length specified by `max`, each of which satisfies the
  indicated `type-spec`"; `max` is a natural or a `defconst` name;
  `:resizable t` allows the resize function, which shortens by dropping or
  extends with the initial value, and "Array resizing is relatively slow,
  so we recommend using it somewhat sparingly"; "ACL2 currently supports
  only single dimensional arrays in stobjs" [:doc defstobj].
- `(HASH-TABLE test size type-spec)`: "the field is logically an
  association list, initially empty, accessed using function
  `hons-assoc-equal`"; under the hood a raw Lisp hash table with test `EQ`,
  `EQL`, `HONS-EQUAL` or `EQUAL`, and `:initially` gives the default for an
  unbound key; it also generates boundp, get?, remove, count, clear and
  init functions [:doc defstobj].
- A stobj name, an array or hash table of stobjs, or `(STOBJ-TABLE)`:
  section 3.8 [:doc nested-stobjs].
- `(SATISFIES pred)` is allowed only when its guard obligation is closed by
  "the limited simplification used by `verify-guards` with option
  `:guard-simplify :limited`" [:doc defstobj].

The default names, for a field `c`: `cP` recognizes the field; a scalar
field has `(c name)` and `(UPDATE-c v name)`; an array field `(cI i name)`,
`(UPDATE-cI i v name)`, `(c-LENGTH name)` and `(RESIZE-c i name)`; a
hash-table field `(c-get k name)`, `(c-put k v name)`, `c-boundp`, `c-get?`,
`c-rem`, `c-count`, `c-clear`, `c-init`; and for the stobj, `(nameP x)` and
`(CREATE-name)` [:doc defstobj]. A constant `*acc*` is introduced for each
accessor, its value the field's position, "used for certain calls of `nth`
and `update-nth` that are displayed to the user in proof output" -- which
is why checkpoints over stobjs print `(NTH *GET-PC* MACH)`
[:doc defstobj] [measured: the checkpoints in `examples/stobjs/array-stobj.lisp`
while it was being written]. "Evaluation of a `defstobj` event disables the
executable-counterpart of the creator function" [:doc defstobj].

Performance keywords: "The `:inline` keyword argument controls whether or
not the functions introduced are inlined (as macros under the hood, in raw
Lisp), with the exception of the resize function"; the manual reports
inlining reducing time "by a factor of 10 or more" on contrived
array-access examples and "by a factor of at least 2" on realistic ones,
at the price that redefinition and tracing do not see through the macros
[:doc defstobj]. "It can also improve performance to specify
`:non-memoizable t`, which disallows memoization but therefore avoids the
cost of certain 'flushing' operations" [:doc defstobj]. fn's octet buffer
does both, and says why: "No function over this stobj, `fn-octets` or a
congruent clone may be memoized (ACL2 refuses the `memoize` event), so no
update carries the memoize-flush test: one load, compare and branch per
array store" [fn: books/octets-stobj.lisp:72-79 @3bbfc4dca].

Congruent stobjs: "Two stobjs are may be considered to be 'congruent' if
they have the same structure, that is, their `defstobj` events are
identical when ignoring field names"; given `:congruent-to st1`, "ACL2
allows you to substitute one for another in a function call. Any number of
stobjs may be replaced with congruent stobjs in the call, provided no two
get replaced with the same stobj" [:doc defstobj]. A non-executable stobj
has no global instance; "you can avoid allocating such memory when your
intended use of `st` is only as a local stobj ... or as the type of a stobj
field of another stobj" [:doc defstobj].

The example: a machine with a fixed register array, a resizable memory, a
scalar and a hash table, a guard-verified fill loop over the array, and the
read-over-write, length and recognizer-preservation theorems a caller needs
[example: examples/stobjs/array-stobj.lisp]:

```lisp
(defstobj mach
  (regs :type (array (unsigned-byte 32) (*regs*)) :initially 0)
  (mem  :type (array (unsigned-byte 8) (16)) :initially 0 :resizable t)
  (pc   :type (unsigned-byte 32) :initially 0)
  (seen :type (hash-table eql))
  :renaming ((pc get-pc) (update-pc set-pc))
  :inline t)

(defthm regsi-of-update-regsi
  (implies (and (natp i) (natp j))
           (equal (regsi i (update-regsi j v mach))
                  (if (equal i j) v (regsi i mach)))))

(defthm regsp-of-update-nth
  (implies (and (regsp l) (natp j) (< j (len l)) (unsigned-byte-p 32 v))
           (regsp (update-nth j v l))))

(defthm machp-of-update-regsi
  (implies (and (machp mach)
                (natp j) (< j (regs-length mach))
                (unsigned-byte-p 32 v))
           (machp (update-regsi j v mach))))

(in-theory (disable regsi update-regsi regs-length machp))
```

A resize is logically `RESIZE-LIST`, about which nothing is built in; the
book proves `(equal (len (resize-list lst n default)) (nfix n))` before
its length theorem [example: examples/stobjs/array-stobj.lisp].

ACL2 arrays (`aref1`, `aset1`, `compress1`) are a different mechanism: "An
ACL2 1-dimensional array is actually an alist" with a header, made fast by
`compress1`, and "ACL2's single-threaded objects ... provide a similar
functionality that is generally more efficient when there are updates
(writes), but is also more restrictive" [:doc arrays]. This guide does not
use them.

fn's best stobj is its Message-ID index, which shows what the syntax buys
when the representation is chosen for the machine: "a two-level directory
of PAGE stobjs (`fn-mlg`), each a fixed array of 2,048 words", the words
`(unsigned-byte 61)` so that "Every word is a fixnum on SBCL x86-64, so the
accessor returns it unboxed and the scan allocates nothing. MEASURED on
the flat predecessor of this representation (persvati, 2026-10-01,
sb-ext:get-bytes-consed around 10,000 lookups on tables of 10,000
entries): fn-mpxt-candidates 6,364 bytes consed per lookup,
fn-mlh-candidates 16 (the one cons of the result list)"
[fn: books/msgid-linear-exec.lisp:7-37 @3bbfc4dca]. The 2026-10-28 review
calls it "The best representation in the tree"
[fn: build/coordinator/REPRESENTATION-HISTORY-20261028.md:77 @worktree 2026-10-28].

## 3.7 Local stobjs

### What goes wrong

The body of a `with-local-stobj` returns the ordinary values and NOT the
local stobj. fn's lane continuation records this as a pitfall that cost an
attempt: "the body returns the other values and NOT the local stobj:
`(with-local-stobj st (mv-let (answer other st) (CALL ... st) (mv answer
other)))`. A stobj argument must be a variable, never a call: bind with
`let` first" [fn: build/coordinator/lanedumps/cut-m2a-lb-continuation.md:26 @worktree 2026-10-28].
Returning the stobj gets an error that does not mention stobjs at all:
"Contrary to the declaration that it is IGNOREd, the variable HIST is used
in the MV-LET expression that binds TOP, N and HIST"
[measured: the first `must-fail` form of `examples/stobjs/local-stobj.lisp`],
because the expansion declares the stobj ignored after the `mv-let`.

A second trap is specific to attachable stobjs (section 3.10): a fn
builder found that "A compiled defconst/initializer, or any compiled
function, running with-local-stobj of an ATTACHABLE stobj (fn-arena,
fn-cat, fn-hist) compiled plain and loaded/called after its attach book
makes certification ILLEGAL (CHK-ABSSTOBJ-INVARIANTS). The REPL does not
show it unless you query (@ illegal-to-certify-message) at the end"
[cv: ac5fb887#20].

### The mechanism

"`With-local-stobj` can be thought of as a macro, where the example form
above expands as follows":

```lisp
(mv-let (result st)
        (let ((st (create-st)))
          (compute-with-st x st))
        (declare (ignore st))
        result)
```

"However, ACL2 expects you to use `with-local-stobj`, not its expansion.
More precisely, stobj creator functions are only allowed via
`with-local-stobj` or in logic-only situations (like theorems and hints).
Moreover, neither `with-local-stobj` nor its expansions are legal when
typed directly at the top-level loop" [:doc with-local-stobj]. The
requirement on the form: "`mv-let-form` is a call of `mv-let` that binds
`stobj-name` but does not return `stobj-name` -- in fact, if
`mv-let-form` is `(mv-let (...) ... body)`, then `body` does not even
reference `stobj-name`" [:doc with-local-stobj]. "If a stobj `ST` is bound
upon beginning evaluation of a form `(with-local-stobj ST ...)`, then the
value of `ST` is the same immediately before evaluating that form as it is
immediately after that evaluation", and "For technical reasons, ACL2 will
not allow you to enable the `:EXECUTABLE-COUNTERPART` rune of a stobj
creator function" [:doc with-local-stobj].

The example computes the most common byte of a list in a scratch histogram
stobj and returns two values out of it [example: examples/stobjs/local-stobj.lisp]:

```lisp
(defstobj hist
  (bins :type (array (unsigned-byte 60) (256)) :initially 0)
  :inline t
  :non-executable t)

(defun most-common-byte (xs)
  (declare (xargs :guard (byte-listp xs)))
  (with-local-stobj
   hist
   (mv-let (top n hist)
     (let ((hist (count-bytes xs hist)))
       (mv-let (top n)
         (most-common-byte-loop 0 0 0 hist)
         (mv top n hist)))
     (mv top n))))

(assert-event
 (equal (mv-list 2 (most-common-byte '(1 2 2 3 2 9 1)))
        '(2 3)))
```

`:non-executable t` avoids allocating a global `hist` that nothing uses
[:doc defstobj]. The `assert-event` reads two values with `mv-list`; a bare
call is refused with "This function call returns a result of shape (MV *
*) where a result of shape * is required" [measured: the first draft of
that assertion]. The guard proof of `most-common-byte` needs the theorem
that `count-bytes` returns a `histp`, which the book proves first.

A local stobj is also how a stobj is used as a memo table inside a pure
function; the manual: "`With-local-stobj` can be useful when a stobj is
used to memoize intermediate results during a computation, yet it is
desired not to make the `stobj` a formal parameter for the function and its
callers" [:doc with-local-stobj], and `books/demos/defabsstobj-example-2.lisp`
does it with an abstract stobj (`fib-fast`) [books: demos/defabsstobj-example-2.lisp:314-321].

## 3.8 Nested stobjs and `stobj-let`

### What goes wrong

A stobj-typed field cannot be read with its accessor. The reason is
aliasing: logically `(let* ((child (fld2 parent)) (child (update-fld 3
child))) (mv child parent))` leaves `parent` unchanged, but "stobjs are
updated with destructive assignments. That is, we really are updating
`(fld2 parent)` to be the new value of `child`, whether this is explained
logically or not. ... Thus, ACL2 disallows direct calls of stobj accessors
and updaters for fields whose `:type` is a stobj or an array or hash table
of stobjs (or a stobj-table). Instead, ACL2 provides `stobj-let`"
[:doc nested-stobjs].

The variables `stobj-let` binds must be stobj NAMES. Binding three children
of type `acct` at once as `acct`, `acct2` and `fees` was refused: "The
stobj-let bound variable FEES is not the name of a known single-threaded
object in the current ACL2 world" [measured: the first draft of `xfer` in
`examples/stobjs/nested-stobj.lisp`]. Three children of one type need
three congruent stobj names.

The guard proofs through `stobj-let` are the stobj guard proofs of section
3.6, one level deeper: every child accessor and updater, every parent
accessor and updater, closed after its read-over-write facts are proved
[example: examples/stobjs/nested-stobj.lisp].

### The mechanism

The general form and its expansion [:doc nested-stobjs]:

```lisp
(stobj-let
 BINDINGS
 PRODUCER-VARIABLES
 PRODUCER
 CONSUMER)
```

"For `PRODUCER-VARIABLES` = `(PRODUCER-VAR)`:

```lisp
(let BINDINGS'
  (declare (ignorable . STOBJ-LET-BOUND-VARIABLES))
  (let ((PRODUCER-VAR PRODUCER))
    (let* UPDATES
      CONSUMER)))
```

Otherwise" the inner `let` is an `mv-let` over the producer variables
[:doc nested-stobjs]. Each binding is `(VAR ACCESSOR)` or `(VAR ACCESSOR
UPDATER)`, the accessor of a field of one parent; the updates write back
every producer variable that is a bound child. The restrictions that make
it sound: "`ST` must not occur free in `PRODUCER` when at least one
variable in `STOBJ-LET-BOUND-VARIABLES` occurs in `PRODUCER`; and every
variable in `STOBJ-LET-BOUND-VARIABLES` must not occur free in `CONSUMER`"
[:doc nested-stobjs]. When two bindings may access the same array or hash
table element and one is a producer variable, the translation adds a
`<check>` with "an extra guard proof obligation, which guarantees that no
aliasing occurs"; `:trans1` does not show it, `(untranslate (body 'FN nil
(w state)) nil (w state))` does [:doc nested-stobjs]. A `stobj-let`
"cannot be evaluated directly in the top-level loop" [:doc nested-stobjs].
For memoization: "the raw Lisp code generated for `stobj-let` will clear
the memoization table for every function taking the parent stobj as an
input, if any child stobj bound in the bindings is among the producer
variables -- unless the parent stobj was introduced by `defstobj` using
keyword argument `:non-memoizable t`" [:doc nested-stobjs].

The example is a bank: a child stobj for fees, an array of four account
children, and a transfer that binds two array elements and the fee child
at once [example: examples/stobjs/nested-stobj.lisp]:

```lisp
(defstobj acct (bal :type (integer 0 *) :initially 0))
(defstobj acct2 (bal2 :type (integer 0 *) :initially 0) :congruent-to acct)
(defstobj acct3 (bal3 :type (integer 0 *) :initially 0) :congruent-to acct)

(defstobj bank
  (fees :type acct)
  (accts :type (array acct (4)))
  (nops :type (integer 0 *) :initially 0))

(defun xfer (i j amt bank)
  (declare (xargs :stobjs bank
                  :guard (and (natp i) (< i (accts-length bank))
                              (natp j) (< j (accts-length bank))
                              (not (equal i j))
                              (natp amt))
                  :guard-hints (("Goal" :use (...)))))
  (stobj-let
   ((acct (acctsi i bank))
    (acct2 (acctsi j bank))
    (acct3 (fees bank) update-fees))
   (acct acct2 acct3)
   (let* ((have (bal acct))
          (moved (if (<= (+ 1 amt) have) amt 0))
          (fee (if (< 0 moved) 1 0))
          (acct (update-bal (- have (+ moved fee)) acct))
          (acct2 (update-bal (+ moved (bal acct2)) acct2))
          (acct3 (update-bal (+ fee (bal acct3)) acct3)))
     (mv acct acct2 acct3))
   (update-nops (+ 1 (nops bank)) bank)))
```

The `(not (equal i j))` conjunct is the aliasing condition: two bindings of
elements of one array, both producer variables. Logically a `stobj-let` is
the `let` above, so the parent's read-over-write theorems are provable as
for any list (`balance-of-deposit` in the book).

Abstract stobjs can have child stobjs too, declared with an export's
`:updater` keyword; "A child stobj accessor/updater pair may be used in
`stobj-let` in the same way when the parent is an abstract stobj as when
the parent is a concrete stobj", the aliasing checks being made "with
respect to the underlying concrete child stobj accessors"
[:doc nested-stobjs]. fn's paged catalog uses `stobj-let` this way: "every
table operation is the old executable called through `stobj-let`"
[fn: books/catalog-paged.lisp:37-41 @3bbfc4dca].

### `swap-stobjs` and stobj tables

"The macro call `(swap-stobjs st1 st2)` is allowed when `st1` and `st2` are
congruent stobjs. The logical meaning is simply to return the two stobjs in
reverse order ... after this call of `swap-stobjs`, the new value of stobj
`st1` is considered to be the old value of `st2`" [:doc swap-stobjs];
"`swap-stobjs` calls must not be made directly in the top-level loop"
[:doc swap-stobjs]. A `stobj-table` field "may be viewed as an association
list mapping stobj names to corresponding stobjs", accessed through
`stobj-let` with `(ST (TBL-GET 'ST PARENT (CREATE-ST)))`, where "the first
argument of the accessor must be the quotation of the name of the bound
stobj ... and the third argument must be a call of that stobj's creator
function"; its recognizer is `t` and the per-key invariant is maintained in
raw Lisp only [:doc stobj-table]. The example has a double buffer flipped
by `swap-stobjs` and a counter kept in a stobj table, each with a theorem
and an executed check [example: examples/stobjs/swap-and-table.lisp].

## 3.9 Abstract stobjs

### What goes wrong

**An export that updates the foundation twice is refused without
`:protect t`.** "The :EXEC field CTR$C-INCR, specified for defabsstobj field
CTR-INCR, appears capable of modifying the foundational stobj, CTR$C,
non-atomically; yet :PROTECT T was not specified for this field"
[measured: the counter of `examples/stobjs/absstobj-counter.lisp` without
`:protect t`]. The manual's advice is to ignore `:protect` until this error
and then add it; "beware that when `:PROTECT` is `t`, the generated raw
Lisp code runs slightly less efficiently -- though perhaps with negligible
efficiency loss if the `:EXEC` function is not trivial" [:doc defabsstobj].
`books/demos/defabsstobj-example-3.lisp` shows avoiding it when "the IF
structures of relevant function bodies guarantee that only one update is
done on each computation branch" [books: demos/defabsstobj-example-3.lisp:5-12].
fn's octet buffer writes the atomicity argument beside each export that
goes without: "Atomicity (no :protect): the exec is one array store and
touches no fill, so an interrupt leaves the concrete stobj equal to the
state before or after the store, and both correspond to the logic"
[fn: books/octets-stobj.lisp:1167-1170 @3bbfc4dca].

**The event fails until every obligation is in the world, and it prints
them.** Submitting a counter with no obligations proved:

```
ACL2 Error in ( DEFABSSTOBJ CTR ...):  The following events must be
admitted before the given defabsstobj event is admitted.  See :DOC
defabsstobj.

(DEFTHM CREATE-CTR{CORRESPONDENCE}
  (CTR$CORR (CREATE-CTR$C) (CREATE-CTR$A))
  :RULE-CLASSES NIL)

(DEFTHM CREATE-CTR{PRESERVED}
  (CTR$AP (CREATE-CTR$A))
  :RULE-CLASSES NIL)

(DEFTHM CTR-GET{CORRESPONDENCE}
  (IMPLIES (AND (CTR$CORR CTR$C CTR) (CTR$AP CTR))
           (EQUAL (CTR$C-VAL CTR$C)
                  (CTR$A-GET CTR)))
  :RULE-CLASSES NIL)

(DEFTHM CTR-INCR{CORRESPONDENCE}
  (IMPLIES (AND (CTR$CORR CTR$C CTR) (CTR$AP CTR))
           (CTR$CORR (CTR$C-INCR CTR$C)
                     (CTR$A-INCR CTR)))
  :RULE-CLASSES NIL)

(DEFTHM CTR-INCR{PRESERVED}
  (IMPLIES (CTR$AP CTR)
           (CTR$AP (CTR$A-INCR CTR)))
  :RULE-CLASSES NIL)
```

[measured: a one-field counter, the forms of that error, at the REPL]. No
`{GUARD-THM}` is printed there, because the exec function's guard follows
from the logic's; one is printed when it does not, as for the array
memory's `lookup{guard-thm}`, whose conclusion is `(AND (NATP I) (< I
(MEM$C-AR-LENGTH MEM$C)))` [example: examples/stobjs/attach-mem.lisp].

**The recognizer is still a hypothesis of theorems that depend on it.** The
logic of an abstract stobj primitive is a total function applied to
arbitrary objects in a theorem. `(ctr-val (ctr-incr-n n ctr))` equals `(+
n (ctr-val ctr))` only for a `ctrp` counter: without that hypothesis the
proof fails at `n = 0` with the checkpoint `(ACL2-NUMBERP (CAR CTR))`
[example: examples/stobjs/absstobj-counter.lisp]. What the `{PRESERVED}`
obligations buy is the guard side: callers do not re-establish the
recognizer at run time.

**Attachments may not reach the recognizer or the correspondence.** A
soundness bug "that existed through ACL2 Version_8.6" is closed by two
restrictions: "No supporter of a stobj recognizer is allowed to have an
attachment", and "For a `defabsstobj` event, there must
be a correspondence function whose supporters are not allowed to have
attachments", met either by `:CORR-FN-EXISTS t` with an attachment-free
correspondence or by attachment-free supporters of every primitive that
returns the stobj [:doc stobj-attachment-restrictions]. fn hit this when the
catalog's recognizer came to reach its digest function: "the tree learned
that when the catalog's recognizer reached `fn-digest` (books/catalog.lisp,
2026-09-27) and no book could include it beside crypto-attach"; its
generator now checks "the ancestors of what the recognizer and
correspondence will call ... in the world BEFORE anything is generated"
[fn: books/def-representation.lisp:19-28 @3bbfc4dca].

**`:LOGIC` function names are substituted in guards.** The guard of an
export is derived from its logic function's guard by replacing each
primitive's `:LOGIC` function with the primitive itself, so "a good
discipline is to define `:LOGIC` functions that are not intended for
general use, but are intended only for use as `:LOGIC` functions of
corresponding stobj primitives. For example, suppose that you use `length`
as the `:LOGIC` function for some stobj primitive ... Then every call of
`length` will be replaced by `f` when creating the guard of a stobj
primitive" [:doc defabsstobj].

### The mechanism

An abstract stobj gives an existing stobj, its foundation, a new logical
interface. The motivation is the cost of the invariant: with a concrete
stobj and a predicate `good-stp`, "When `foo` is executed on concrete data
in the ACL2 loop, the guard check may be expensive because `(good-stp st)`
is expensive" and "Reasoning about `foo` ... involves proving hypotheses of
invariance theorems, which may be complicated for the user to manage or
slow for the theorem prover" [:doc defabsstobj]. Goel, Hunt and Kaufmann:
"When introducing an abstract stobj, we prove once and for all that it
remains in 'lockstep' correspondence with its associated concrete stobj.
Thus, the user can define a simpler logical representation of the concrete
stobj in order to abstract away its complexity for reasoning"
[paper: goel2013-acl2-abstract-stobjs-isa-modeling.txt, section 1]. The
manual's picture:

```
 Abstract               u$a1       u$a2       u$a3
 (:logic)         st$a0  --> st$a1  --> st$a2  -->   ...

                    ^          ^          ^               ^
 Correspondence     |          |          |          ...  |
                    v          v          v               v

                        u$c1       u$c2       u$c3
 Foundation       st$c0  --> st$c1  --> st$c2  -->   ...
 (:exec)
```

[:doc defabsstobj]

The three kinds of obligation, in the manual's words [:doc defabsstobj]:

- `{CORRESPONDENCE}`: "These guarantee that evaluation in the logic agrees
  with evaluation using live stobjs". For the creator, the correspondence
  holds initially. For an export that returns other than the stobj, the
  `:LOGIC` and `:EXEC` results are `EQUAL` under the correspondence and the
  logic guard; for one that returns the stobj, "the conclusion uses the
  correspondence function instead of `EQUAL`"; "For exported functions that
  return multiple values, such conclusions are conjoined together over the
  returned values".
- `{PRESERVED}`: "guarantee that updates to the abstract stobj preserve its
  recognizer. The fact that every exported function has this property
  provides justification for an optimization performed by ACL2 during
  generation of proof obligations for guard verification, by assuming that
  the recognizer always holds".
- `{GUARD-THM}`: "they state that if the `:LOGIC` function's guard holds,
  then the `:EXEC` function's guard holds".

"Although the `defabsstobj` event will fail if the required lemmas have not
been proved, first it will print the `defthm` forms that must be admitted
... you are welcome to supply whatever `:rule-classes` you prefer, even
though the system creates `:rule-classes nil` by default" [:doc defabsstobj].
"Note that a `defabsstobj` event will fail if the required lemmas ... have
not been proved, unless proofs are being skipped. The exemption when
skipping proofs allows the supporting lemmas to be `local` to books and
`encapsulate` events" [:doc defabsstobj]. And the manual's example adds
in passing: "we do not say in the comments below that every `:LOGIC` and
`:EXEC` function must be guard-verified, but that is indeed a requirement"
[:doc defabsstobj].

The general form [:doc defabsstobj]:

```lisp
(defabsstobj st
  :foundation foundation
  :recognizer recognizer
  :creator creator
  :corr-fn corr-fn
  :corr-fn-exists corr-fn-exists
  :congruent-to congruent-to
  :non-executable non-executable
  :protect-default protect-default
  :attachable att
  :exports (e1 ... ek))
```

Two remarks from the summary. "Unlike `defstobj`, there is no `:renaming`
argument ... there is no `:inline` or `:non-memoizable` argument;
`:inline` is essentially t, in the sense that stobj primitives are macros
in raw Lisp; and the `:non-memoizable` argument is derived implicitly, to
agree with non-memoizability of the foundational stobj" [:doc defabsstobj].
And a congruent abstract stobj skips the obligation checks: "if it is not
`nil`, then the checks for lemmas -- `{CORRESPONDENCE}`, `{GUARD-THM}`, and
`{PRESERVED}` -- are omitted" [:doc defabsstobj].

The complete counter. The logical value is a pair (value . operations);
the foundation is a two-field stobj; increment and reset each update both
fields, so each is `:protect t`; every obligation is written as ACL2 prints
it and proved before the event [example: examples/stobjs/absstobj-counter.lisp]:

```lisp
(defstobj ctr$c
  (ctr$c-val :type (integer 0 *) :initially 0)
  (ctr$c-ops :type (integer 0 *) :initially 0))

(defun ctr$ap (x)
  (declare (xargs :guard t))
  (and (consp x) (natp (car x)) (natp (cdr x))))
(defun create-ctr$a () (declare (xargs :guard t)) (cons 0 0))
(defun ctr$a-val (x) (declare (xargs :guard (ctr$ap x))) (car x))
(defun ctr$a-ops (x) (declare (xargs :guard (ctr$ap x))) (cdr x))
(defun ctr$a-incr (x)
  (declare (xargs :guard (ctr$ap x)))
  (cons (+ 1 (car x)) (+ 1 (cdr x))))
(defun ctr$a-reset (x)
  (declare (xargs :guard (ctr$ap x)))
  (cons 0 (+ 1 (cdr x))))

(defun ctr$c-incr (ctr$c)
  (declare (xargs :stobjs ctr$c))
  (let ((ctr$c (update-ctr$c-val (+ 1 (ctr$c-val ctr$c)) ctr$c)))
    (update-ctr$c-ops (+ 1 (ctr$c-ops ctr$c)) ctr$c)))
; ctr$c-reset likewise

(defun-nx ctr$corr (ctr$c x)
  (and (equal (ctr$c-val ctr$c) (car x))
       (equal (ctr$c-ops ctr$c) (cdr x))))

(defthm create-ctr{correspondence}
  (ctr$corr (create-ctr$c) (create-ctr$a))
  :rule-classes nil)
(defthm create-ctr{preserved}
  (ctr$ap (create-ctr$a))
  :rule-classes nil)
(defthm ctr-val{correspondence}
  (implies (and (ctr$corr ctr$c ctr) (ctr$ap ctr))
           (equal (ctr$c-val ctr$c) (ctr$a-val ctr)))
  :rule-classes nil)
(defthm ctr-incr{correspondence}
  (implies (and (ctr$corr ctr$c ctr) (ctr$ap ctr))
           (ctr$corr (ctr$c-incr ctr$c) (ctr$a-incr ctr)))
  :rule-classes nil)
(defthm ctr-incr{preserved}
  (implies (ctr$ap ctr) (ctr$ap (ctr$a-incr ctr)))
  :rule-classes nil)
; ... ctr-ops{correspondence}, ctr-reset{correspondence}, ctr-reset{preserved}

(defabsstobj ctr
  :foundation ctr$c
  :recognizer (ctrp :logic ctr$ap :exec ctr$cp)
  :creator (create-ctr :logic create-ctr$a :exec create-ctr$c)
  :corr-fn ctr$corr
  :exports ((ctr-val :logic ctr$a-val :exec ctr$c-val)
            (ctr-ops :logic ctr$a-ops :exec ctr$c-ops)
            (ctr-incr :logic ctr$a-incr :exec ctr$c-incr :protect t)
            (ctr-reset :logic ctr$a-reset :exec ctr$c-reset :protect t)))
```

After the event, theorems are about the pair, callers are ordinary
single-threaded functions, and a pure function can use a local counter
(`count-to`, checked at 1000).

### Generating the obligations

The obligations are mechanical once the correspondence and its lemmas
exist, and ACL2 will hand them over as data. "`Defabsstobj-missing-events`
is a macro ... This macro has the same syntax as `defabsstobj` ... The
result is an error triple `(mv erp val state)`. If `erp` is `nil`, then
`val` is the list of all objects `(name formula . old-formula)`, where a
`defthm` event named `name` remains to be admitted whose translated formula
is `formula`" [:doc defabsstobj-missing-events]. fn's generators admit
them this way, "obtained from ACL2 itself by `defabsstobj-missing-events`
and closed by one uniform hint" [fn: books/def-representation.lisp:13-17 @3bbfc4dca],
with a helper of six lines:

```lisp
(defun adt-obligation-thms (missing hints wrld)
  (if (endp missing)
      nil
    (cons `(defthm ,(car (car missing))
             ,(untranslate (cadr (car missing)) t wrld)
             :rule-classes nil
             :hints ,hints)
          (adt-obligation-thms (cdr missing) hints wrld))))
```

[fn: books/proto/adt.lisp:266-273 @3bbfc4dca]. The example does the same
for a bounded set of naturals below 16, whose foundation is a bit array and
a cardinality cell. The defabsstobj form is a constant, so the generator
and the event cannot drift apart; the outer `make-event` splices the
constant's arguments into the macro call and the inner one runs it
[example: examples/stobjs/absstobj-generated.lisp]:

```lisp
(defconst *bset-defabsstobj*
  '(defabsstobj bset
     :foundation bset$c
     :recognizer (bsetp :logic bset$ap :exec bset$cp)
     :creator (create-bset :logic create-bset$a :exec create-bset$c)
     :corr-fn bset$corr
     :exports ((bset-mem :logic bset$a-mem :exec bset$c-mem)
               (bset-insert :logic bset$a-insert :exec bset$c-insert :protect t)
               (bset-card :logic bset$a-card :exec cnt))))

(make-event
 `(make-event
   (er-let* ((missing (defabsstobj-missing-events ,@(cdr *bset-defabsstobj*))))
     (value (cons 'progn
                  (bset-obligation-thms
                   missing
                   '(("Goal" :in-theory (enable bset$corr bset$ap bset$a-mem
                                                bset$a-insert bset$a-card
                                                bset$c-mem bset$c-insert)))
                   (w state)))))))

(make-event *bset-defabsstobj*)
```

The content is in two lemmas about the correspondence's recursion
(`bits-match-reads`, `bits-match-of-insert`), and the recursion is disabled
before the generator runs. With it open, the `bset-mem{correspondence}`
obligation unrolled at index 0 and stopped at a checkpoint about
`(BITS-MATCH 1 (CAR BSET$C) BSET)` [measured: the book with the `(in-theory
(disable bits-match))` removed]. A uniform hint closes the obligations only
when the content is in lemmas and the correspondence is closed.

### When it pays

An abstract stobj pays when one invariant relates several concrete tables
to one simple logical value, and nothing on the served path should ever
check it. fn's catalog is the case: its logical value "is the committed
history as the theorems already know it: a true list of held records"; its
executable is the indexed tables; "The abstraction relation `fn-cat$corr`
states every cross-field invariant the tables must keep against that list,
once; `defabsstobj` proves it established by the creator and preserved by
every export; nothing on a served path evaluates it"
[fn: books/catalog-logic.lisp:4-18 @3bbfc4dca]. After the event, a theorem
over `fn-cat` "is a theorem over the list of held records": `fn-cat-count`
is `len`, `fn-cat-at` is `nth`, `fn-cat-commit` is `append` of one row
[fn: books/catalog.lisp:66-115 @3bbfc4dca].

The memory saving can be large. A lane brief relays the paged catalog's
measurement over 100k rows: "paged 575 B + 2 heap objects/row retained vs
old 1,580 B + 83 objects, reads equal; BUT commit conses 61 KB vs 22 KB
from the tree encode" [cv: a5779663#0]. The second half of that sentence
is the usual price: the obligations prove the representations agree, not
that the new one is cheaper on every path.

### When it traps you

Three fn wounds, each with its record.

**Hand-written obligations.** The 2026-10-06 strategy review counted
"`def-representation` 1, against 285 hand abstract-stobj obligations (~5.8K
lines)" [fn: build/coordinator/STRATEGY-20261006.md:267 @worktree 2026-10-28].
The catalog book itself grew from 1,698 lines when it landed to 4,881 three
days later, before the obligations were split out into `catalog-logic` and
the generic shrank to 208 lines [measured: `for c in $(git log --format=%h
origin/dev -- books/catalog.lisp); do git show $c:books/catalog.lisp | wc
-l; done` in `~/dev/fn`, at `27f583e84` (2026-09-26), `5e990496e`
(2026-09-29) and `9ceb6b82a` (2026-10-01)]. The obligations section of
`catalog-logic.lisp` is introduced as "The obligations, each as
`defabsstobj-missing-events` states it" [fn: books/catalog-logic.lisp:2372 @3bbfc4dca],
and a lane relayed "all 35 obligations at the view" for the paged
implementation alone [cv: a5779663#0]. If the obligations can be printed,
they can be generated; write the generator before the second instance.

**Foundations stacked instead of replaced.** The paged catalog proved its
obligations "through the old implementation: the VIEW of a paged state is
the old foundation with its rows array replaced by the decoded columns ...
each paged executable SIMULATES the old one on the view exactly ... and
every obligation `defabsstobj` states is the old obligation instantiated at
the view. No index theorem is reproved" [fn: books/catalog-paged.lisp:43-50 @3bbfc4dca].
That is an efficient proof. The price is in the foundation: its tables are
"the old foundation `fn-cat$c` NESTED whole, its rows array repurposed as
the overflow cells, and every table operation is the old executable called
through `stobj-let`" [fn: books/catalog-paged.lisp:37-41 @3bbfc4dca], with
a field `(fn-cat$p-tab :type fn-cat$c)` and a scalar kept "so the view is
the old state exactly" [fn: books/catalog-paged.lisp:116-144 @3bbfc4dca].
The 2026-10-28 review's summary: "each stage added a foundation beside the
last (D41's 'one representation' became three)"
[fn: build/coordinator/REPRESENTATION-HISTORY-20261028.md:175 @worktree 2026-10-28].

**A second representation beside the abstract one.** The parked M2(a) lane
added columnar catalog storage as "a standalone defstobj, not an attachment
of fn-cat, so every read arm needs a new fn-lbc argument threaded through
about 50 fn-cat-taking defuns and about 20 proved books, while a
write-side bridge keeps two representations in step. Route A added books
and could delete none until the last reader moved. fn2 should have ONE
catalog representation from the bottom"
[fn: build/coordinator/lanedumps/cut-m2a-lb-continuation.md:13 @worktree 2026-10-28].
The same note found that because `fn-cat$a-at` returns the full held row,
whose home is the log, "a columnar concrete of fn-cat" is "only possible by
storing the held row in a pool and decoding per call, which IS the tree
codec the cut meant to delete"; hence "Decide up front which representation
owns the held row; do not keep two"
[fn: build/coordinator/lanedumps/cut-m2a-lb-continuation.md:12 @worktree 2026-10-28].

The shape to copy, then: one abstract stobj per logical value; one
foundation, replaced rather than nested when it changes; the correspondence
stated once and its content in lemmas; the obligations generated; and the
logical exports chosen so that every implementation you will want can
answer them.

## 3.10 Attachable stobjs

### What goes wrong

The order is fixed and the three events need to be in separate books. "The
implementation stobj, IMPL, is defined before the attach-stobj event
specifying that it will be attached to the attachable stobj, ST. The
attach-stobj event precedes the defabsstobj event that defines ST"
[paper: kaufmann2025-acl2-mutable-objects-several-implementations.txt,
section 2]. A one-book version of the example -- implementation, then
`attach-stobj`, then the attachable `defabsstobj`, all in one book --
passed certification's first pass and then failed when `certify-book`
re-included the book: "HARD ACL2 ERROR in ASSERT$: Assertion failed ...
ACL2 Error [Evaluation] in ( DEFABSSTOBJ MEM ...): Evaluation aborted ...
HARD ACL2 ERROR in ( DEFABSSTOBJ MEM ...): An error has occurred while
DEFABSSTOBJ was defining the supporting functions. This is supposed to be
impossible! Please report this error to the ACL2 implementors"
[measured: `examples/certify.sh stobjs/attach-stobj.lisp` on the one-book
version, with and without `:non-executable t` on the implementation, ACL2
8.7, SBCL 2.6.8]. Split as the community demo splits it (implementation
book; attachable book; a third book that includes the first, attaches, and
includes the second), it certifies [example: examples/stobjs/attach-stobj.lisp].
fn's catalog uses a third arrangement, the implementation in an included
book and the `attach-stobj` and attachable `defabsstobj` together in one
book [fn: books/catalog.lisp:14-17 @3bbfc4dca]; this guide did not test it.

The certification trap of section 3.7 also belongs here: a compiled
function running `with-local-stobj` of an attachable stobj, loaded after
its attach book, makes certification illegal, silently at the REPL
[cv: ac5fb887#20].

### The mechanism

`attach-stobj` "documents a way to modify the foundation and primitives of
an abstract stobj, `gen`, that is introduced by `defabsstobj` using the
keyword argument `:attachable t`. ... Execution of its primitives can be
provided by corresponding primitives of a specified abstract stobj,
`impl`" [:doc attach-stobj]. "The guiding principle is that `gen` is
modified by its attachment to `impl` so that `gen` behaves exactly as
`impl` except for the names introduced and the `:non-executable`
keyword"; the two must have "corresponding logical skeletons": "The
`:exports` keyword argument of each must be of the same length,
establishing a positional one-one correspondence between them.
Corresponding exports (i.e., exports in the same position) must have the
same `:logic` functions" [:doc attach-stobj]. "There is no indirection: in
raw Lisp, each primitive macroexpands to a call of the `:exec` primitive of
the attachment", the trade-off being that a function defined during
`include-book` whose body calls an attachable primitive "will be compiled
at that time" [:doc attach-stobj].

Why it exists: "different ACL2 sessions can specify different ways to
execute operations on ST without the need to recertify the book that
defines ST. In particular, those overriding executions can be available
without re-proving the theorems that have been proved about ST"
[paper: kaufmann2025-acl2-mutable-objects-several-implementations.txt,
section 2]. The paper's measurement on the community bigmem models (100,000
writes into a range of 2^30 addresses): the symmetric model 2.75 s for both
low and high addresses, the asymmetric 0.00 s low and 87.91 s high, and
the asymmetric attached to the symmetric 0.00 s and 89.04 s, "the
performance penalties are minor for using attached instead of asymmetric"
[paper: kaufmann2025-acl2-mutable-objects-several-implementations.txt,
section 3].

The example: one logical memory of 64 cells, proved once against an array
foundation, executed through a hash-table implementation
[example: examples/stobjs/attach-stobj.lisp]:

```lisp
; attach-mem-ht.lisp: MEM-HT, the same :logic functions over a hash table,
; every obligation proved; :non-executable t, since it exists to be attached.
; attach-mem.lisp: MEM over an array, :attachable t, and its theorems.

; attach-stobj.lisp:
(include-book "attach-mem-ht")
(attach-stobj mem mem-ht)
(include-book "attach-mem")

(assert-event
 (and (eq (cdr (assoc-eq 'mem (table-alist 'attach-stobj-table (w state))))
          'mem-ht)
      (let* ((gen (access absstobj-info (getpropc 'mem 'absstobj-info nil (w state))
                          :absstobj-tuples))
             (impl (access absstobj-info (getpropc 'mem-ht 'absstobj-info nil (w state))
                           :absstobj-tuples)))
        (equal (caddr (assoc-eq 'create-mem gen))
               (caddr (assoc-eq 'create-mem-ht impl))))))
```

The assertion is the check fn's catalog makes ("OW1: this generic is
introduced only with its concrete implementation") [fn: books/catalog.lisp:50-64 @3bbfc4dca].
The theorem `lookup-update`, proved in `attach-mem.lisp` about the array,
is in the world unchanged, and `poke-and-peek` runs on the live stobj.

One proof detail in the hash-table implementation is a general rewriting
lesson: its key lemma had to be stated as `(equal (equal (cdr
(hons-assoc-equal k alist)) (nth k lst)) t)`, the form the community demo
uses. As a plain `(equal (cdr (hons-assoc-equal k alist)) (nth k lst))`
the left side does not mention `lst`, which then is a free variable that
the hypothesis `(< k (len lst))` must bind, and does not; the obligation's
checkpoint kept the equality [example: examples/stobjs/attach-mem-ht.lisp]
[books: demos/attach-stobj/mem_ht.lisp:73-81].

fn uses attachment differently from its purpose. Its generics are
introduced together with their one implementation, and a 2026-10-13 change
was titled "one-world: each attachable generic exists only attached"
(commit `8434f025f`, `git log origin/dev -- books/catalog.lisp` in fn); the
history columns book does the same with `fn-hist-paged`
[fn: books/history-columns.lisp:13-31 @3bbfc4dca]. That forgoes the
per-session choice of implementation that attach-stobj exists for, while
keeping the cost of an attachable stobj's compile-time behaviour. When
there is one implementation, an ordinary abstract stobj over it is simpler.

## 3.11 Memoization

### What goes wrong

Memoization is a property of raw-Lisp execution, so everything that keeps
execution out of raw Lisp keeps memoization off. "Only the corresponding
raw Lisp function is actually memoized, so guard violations can defeat
memoization, and `:logic` mode functions without their guards verified
will only be memoized when called by `:program` mode functions" [:doc memoize].
The manual's log shows an unverified `fib` memoized with `:ideal-okp t`
staying slow at the top level, because the top level runs the executable
counterpart [:doc memoize].

Some functions cannot be memoized at all: "`Memoize` is illegal for a
function if its arguments include `state`; if it returns any stobjs ... A
constrained function ... cannot be memoized" [:doc memoize]. The refusal
for a stobj-returning function reads "Illegal attempt to set memoize-table:
SET-CELL returns a stobj, CELL (illegal except for profiling)"
[measured: `(memoize 'set-cell)` as in `examples/modelling/memoize.lisp`].
A stobj may be an input, "but in that case, the memoization table for that
stobj will be cleared every time that stobj is updated" [:doc memoize];
"such flushing happens even when a stobj that is congruent to one of its
stobj inputs is updated. For that purpose, an abstract stobj is considered
to be congruent to its foundational stobj" [:doc defabsstobj].

### The mechanism

"When a call of the memoized function is evaluated, the result is
'memoized' by associating the call's arguments with that result, in a
suitable table. But first an attempt is made to avoid such evaluation, by
doing a lookup in that table on the given arguments for the result"
[:doc memoize]. With `:condition`, "An attempt will be made to define a
condition function whose guard and formal parameters list are the same as
those of the memoized function, and whose body is the result ... of
evaluating the given `condition`", named with the suffix
`-MEMOIZE-CONDITION`, and "the memoized function attempts a lookup or table
store if and only if the result of that evaluation is non-`nil`"
[:doc memoize]. `:recursive nil` memoizes top-level calls only; `:aokp`
governs whether results computed with attachments are stored [:doc memoize].
`(unmemoize fn)` turns it off and "To remove the effects of all `memoize`
events, evaluate: `(clear-memo-table)`" [:doc unmemoize]. The hons
background: "`hons` is just `cons`" logically, while under the hood it
returns a normed pair, "much slower than `cons`, even when given normed
arguments" [:doc hons]; a fast alist is an alist with a hidden hash table
under a discipline that is not syntactically enforced, where "The only
penalty for a failure to keep track of the most recent extension is a loss
of execution speed, not of correctness" and there is "no automatic
mechanism for reclaiming the hash tables", hence `fast-alist-free`
[:doc fast-alists]. The idea and its history are in [:doc hons-and-memoization].

The example memoizes a guard-verified `fib` with a condition
[example: examples/modelling/memoize.lisp]:

```lisp
(defun fib (n)
  (declare (xargs :guard (natp n)))
  (mbe :logic (cond ((zp n) 0)
                    ((= n 1) 1)
                    (t (+ (fib (- n 1)) (fib (- n 2)))))
       :exec (if (< n 2) n (+ (fib (- n 1)) (fib (- n 2))))))

(memoize 'fib :condition '(> n 10))

(assert-event (equal (fib 90) 2880067194370816120))
```

Measured at the REPL: `(time$ (fib 32))` 0.06 s before `memoize`, 0.00 s
after; `(fib 90)` 0.00 s memoized [measured: the `fib` above with `time$`].
Memoization changed no theorem; `fib-of-2` is proved after the `memoize`
event exactly as before it.

## 3.12 What to take from this chapter

- Close what you define: records under an internals theory, stobj
  accessors after their read-over-write lemmas, recognizers after their
  preservation lemmas, correspondence recursions after their content
  lemmas. A goal that has opened your vocabulary has left your rules
  behind.
- A guard is a run-time contract and a proof obligation, never a
  hypothesis. State what a theorem needs in its hypotheses; prove what a
  guard needs in `:rule-classes nil` lemmas named in `:guard-hints`.
- An `mbe` twin is safe because nothing calls its `:logic` side at run
  time. A twin that is a second callable function is two paths.
- A stobj is a representation decision; an abstract stobj is that decision
  plus one correspondence. Make each once, generate the obligations, and
  replace a foundation rather than nest it.
